A short note on a decomposition that is worth keeping in mind whenever a variational bound is reported as though it were a likelihood.

## The exact statement

For a latent variable model $$p_\theta(x, z)$$ and any variational family $$q_\phi(z \mid x)$$,

$$
\log p_\theta(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\right]}_{\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;x)} + \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_\phi(z \mid x) \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\right)}_{\text{the gap}} .
$$

This is an identity, not an inequality. The bound $$\log p_\theta(x) \geq \mathcal{L}$$ follows only because the second term is non-negative. Two things follow immediately.

First, maximising $$\mathcal{L}$$ in $$\phi$$ is tightening the bound, and maximising in $$\theta$$ is doing two jobs at once — improving the model *and* moving the true posterior toward whatever $$q_\phi$$ can represent. The optimiser is free to prefer the second, and often does.

Second, the gap is never observed. We report $$\mathcal{L}$$ and treat it as a proxy for $$\log p_\theta(x)$$, but nothing in training tells us how large the discrepancy is for a given $$x$$.

## Posterior collapse is a property of the objective

Split the ELBO the other way:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\left[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\right] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_\phi(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\right).
$$

If the decoder $$p_\theta(x \mid z)$$ is expressive enough to model $$x$$ well without conditioning on $$z$$, the first term is nearly constant in $$z$$ and the second is minimised exactly at $$q_\phi(z \mid x) = p(z)$$. The latent carries no information, and the objective is perfectly happy about it.

This is worth stating plainly: collapse here is not an optimisation failure to be engineered around. It is the objective's preferred solution given a sufficiently powerful decoder. Warm-up schedules and KL annealing work by temporarily changing the objective, which is a reasonable thing to do — but it is worth being clear that this is what they are doing.

## Why I care

The same structure shows up whenever a variational bound is applied along a trajectory rather than to a single observation: the per-step gaps compose, and the quantity being reported drifts further from the quantity we want. Working out how they compose is part of what I am currently thinking about.
